Does Stenwatch's scoring find the vulnerabilities attackers actually use?

A point-in-time backtest against CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue · Stenwatch e7516c9 + uncommitted changes · run 2026-10-09 13:10 · Diljot Singh Johal

1. Summary

What was tested. On six start dates, six months apart, we ranked every CVE that was not yet known to be exploited, using only what was knowable that day. Then we counted how many were added to CISA's known-exploited list over the next six months (257 in all) and how much reading each method needed to find them.

  • The Attend queue is small and efficient, but it is not more complete than the critical list. Attend (EPSS at or above 0.1) holds 6.1% of all CVEs and found 40% of the later-exploited ones (95% interval 34% to 46%). The usual CVSS 9 and above list holds 13.0% and found 36% (30% to 42%). The difference in what they found is not statistically significant (p = 0.329), but Attend does it from a queue 2.1 times smaller, so each CVE read is 2.4 times as likely to be a hit.
  • At the same queue size the Stenwatch ranking is clearly better. Reading down the Stenwatch score for as many CVEs as the critical list holds finds 50% of the exploited CVEs, against 36% for the critical list itself. For a queue the size of Attend, a severity ranking finds only 16% against Attend's 40%.
  • The advantage is at small review budgets, and it reverses in the long tail. After reading the first 1% of the list the Stenwatch score has found 21% against 2% for CVSS; after 10%, 46% against 30%. To reach 80% of the exploited CVEs, CVSS needs 42.5% of the list and the Stenwatch score 58.2%. The two gain curves cross at about 25% of the list: reading further than that, the CVSS ranking is ahead. Overall AUC is 0.744 for CVSS and 0.720 for the Stenwatch score. 45% of the later-exploited CVEs had an EPSS below 0.01 on the origin day, and no probability-led ranking can place them early.
  • Most CISA additions cannot be predicted six months ahead. 62% of the 711 CVEs CISA added during the windows had not even been published on the origin day. They are out of reach of any ranking; they are caught once CISA lists them, which is what Stenwatch's KEV feed is for.

A correction. An earlier exploratory run reported that the Attend queue caught about 70% against about a third for the critical list. That result is withdrawn. It came from a local database that was missing most CVEs published in 2024 and 2025, which silently removed the hardest-to-find exploited CVEs from the test. This report uses a complete database and replaces it. Please do not quote the 70% figure.

Read this before quoting the numbers. 257 positives are enough to separate large differences but not to give tight percentages; the intervals show the uncertainty. The test measures the global scoring signals only. It does not test the KEV flag, ransomware use, threat-group matching or your asset list, which have no point-in-time history. See section 7.

3. Questions and hypotheses

Stenwatch ranks vulnerabilities by risk = likelihood × impact, where likelihood leans on EPSS and impact on CVSS. The question is whether that ranking would have pointed a team at vulnerabilities attackers went on to use, better than the common practice of working down the CVSS list. Two hypotheses were written before the complete-data run:

  • H1. A ranking by EPSS × CVSS finds later-exploited CVEs with less review effort than a ranking by CVSS alone. Verdict: Partly supported. It holds at small review budgets (the first 1%, 5% and 10% of the list) but not over the whole list: CVSS reaches 80% sooner and has the higher AUC.
  • H2. The Attend queue catches more later-exploited CVEs than a critical-severity queue (CVSS 9 and above) of similar or smaller size. Verdict: Partly supported. Attend caught 40% against 36% (not a significant difference) from a queue 2.1 times smaller. "Catches more" is not shown; "catches as many from far less" is.

The first exploratory run suggested stronger results than these (section 1, correction). The windows, thresholds and measures below were fixed before the complete-data run and were not changed afterwards. The thresholds are the product's own defaults (profile.example.yaml), not values tuned on this data. The equal-size comparison (6.5) was added after seeing the first results, because comparing queues of different sizes is unfair to both; treat it as exploratory.

5. Method

5.1 Design

Six consecutive, non-overlapping windows of 180 days (figure 1). At each origin date T0 we rebuild the situation of a team on that day: which CVEs existed, how severe they looked, what EPSS said, and which were already known to be exploited. We then look forward 180 days and see which CVEs were added to KEV. Non-overlapping windows keep one exploited CVE from being counted twice.

5.2 Population and labels

  • Population at T0: every CVE published on or before T0, with an EPSS score on T0, and not yet in KEV on T0. Already-exploited CVEs are excluded because ranking them is not a prediction.
  • Positive: a member of that population added to KEV during the next 180 days. Everything else in the population is a negative.

Of the 711 CVEs added to KEV during the six windows, 257 (36%) are in scope. The rest cannot be scored on the origin day: 444 were published after T0, 9 are not in the NVD copy, and 1 had no EPSS score on T0.

5.3 What is compared

NameHow it works
Stenwatch scoreEPSS × CVSS: the part of the product's risk formula (likelihood × impact) that can be reproduced without KEV, threat-group or asset information. The weights are constants and do not change the order.
EPSS aloneRank by exploit probability.
CVSS aloneRank by severity: what most vulnerability lists do.
Attend queueEPSS at or above 0.1 (the product's Attend threshold for CVEs not yet known to be exploited).
Attend + Track queueEPSS at or above 0.01, or CVSS at or above 7.0.
CVSS 9 and above, CVSS 7 and aboveThe two severity lists teams commonly work from.

5.4 Measures and statistics

  • Recall of a queue and its size (the review cost); hit rate and lift.
  • Gain curve (figure 3): recall against share reviewed while reading down a ranking. Reviews to find 50% and 80% of the positives (median of the six windows). ROC AUC: the chance that a random positive is ranked above a random negative (0.5 is no skill, 1 is perfect).
  • Ties. CVSS has few distinct values, so many CVEs tie. Ties are resolved by their expected value under random order (exact, no random seed); AUC gives half credit to ties.
  • Intervals. Pooled recall uses 95% Wilson score intervals.
  • Paired comparison. Two queues are judged on the same positives, so the difference is tested on the positives only one of them caught, with an exact two-sided binomial (sign) test.
  • Across windows. AUC is the mean ± standard deviation of six windows. No test is applied across windows; six is too few.

6.6 Why severity wins in the tail

45% of the later-exploited CVEs had an EPSS score under 0.01 on the origin day, so they sit in the bottom of any probability ranking and are reached only by reading very far down it. Severity does not depend on prior exploitation signals, which is why it recovers them late. This is the argument for keeping a severity-based second pass behind the Attend queue.

7. Threats to validity and limits

  1. KEV is a proxy for "exploited". CISA lists vulnerabilities with evidence of exploitation that are relevant to its mission and have remediation guidance. Exploited CVEs it never lists count here as negatives. The test measures agreement with CISA's list.
  2. Only CVEs that existed on T0 can be scored. 62% of KEV additions in these windows were published after T0; no ranking could have found them in advance.
  3. Few positives. 257 in total, between 31 and 59 per window. Single-window figures are noisy. Pooled intervals assume positives are independent, but exploited CVEs often cluster in one product, so the true uncertainty is somewhat larger than shown.
  4. EPSS changed model version during the period, so scores are not perfectly comparable across windows; section 8 shows sensitivity to the threshold.
  5. CVSS is today's value. NVD revises scores after publication. If revisions are more likely for CVEs that later prove important, severity is slightly flattered; the bias would favour the CVSS baselines, not Stenwatch.
  6. Not tested here: the KEV flag, ransomware use, threat-group overlap, asset criticality, internet exposure and supplier context. They have no point-in-time history. In use they act on top of the ranking, so this report tests the part that can be tested and nothing more.
  7. Global, not local. The queues cover all CVEs. In use, Stenwatch first filters to your own assets, which shrinks the queue by orders of magnitude; the ranking quality measured here carries over, the percentages do not.
  8. An earlier result was wrong. See the correction in section 1. The self-check on database coverage was added because of it.

10. Conclusions and next steps

  1. Use Attend as the first-pass queue. It is 2.1 times smaller than the critical list, finds about as many later-exploited CVEs, and each CVE read is 2.4 times as likely to matter.
  2. Do not stop at Attend. About 60% of later-exploited CVEs sit outside it. Keep a severity-ordered second pass behind it for as long as capacity allows; the Track tier is that pass, and it is large.
  3. Keep the KEV feed fresh. 62% of CISA additions concern CVEs that did not exist six months earlier; the Act tier, which reacts to KEV, is what covers them.
  4. Re-run every quarter. New windows add positives and show whether EPSS model changes move the thresholds; the commands above take minutes.
  5. Add labels that do not depend on CISA (public exploit code, vendor advisories noting exploitation) to reduce the proxy problem.
  6. Test the local part separately. Asset context cannot be backtested globally; measure it on your own incidents and patch decisions.
